Northern Forests Are Burning Faster, and the Numbers Show It

Two decades of tree cover loss data reveal a sharp recent surge in wildfire damage in Canada and Russia, the two countries hit hardest.

By Noah Ott  ·  Data: Our World in Data, 2001–2024

As the global climate warms, wildfires across Canada and the Pacific coast of the United States seem to be getting worse. Is that impression backed by the numbers, or is it the product of heavier media attention to climate change? A look at global tree cover loss data suggests the trend is real, and that it has accelerated in the most recent years on record.

The analysis draws on Our World in Data's figures for tree cover loss by dominant driver, covering 2001 through 2024. Tracking only the hectares of forest lost to wildfire, the picture is clear: the burden falls on the world's northern forests, and the latest years stand apart from the rest.

Where the fires hit hardest

The interactive map below shows hectares of tree cover lost to wildfire in every country, year by year. Press play and the colors shift from pale to deep red as losses mount.

The map confirms what media coverage suggests: wildfire losses concentrate in cold, dry, heavily forested northern countries, chiefly Canada and Russia. Brazil is a southern outlier, most likely because of its vast area and expanse of forest. For residents of the colder, drier regions most at risk, the data underscores the importance of being prepared for fire.

The most striking pattern is in time. Losses in the last five years of data run well above those of the first five. Canada lost almost 8 million hectares of tree cover in 2023, and Russia lost about 4 million in 2024. In 2001, the two countries together lost less than 2 million. Against that starting point, the 2023 and 2024 figures amount to roughly a doubling to a quadrupling of losses.

A steadier baseline: the United States

Totals alone can overstate or understate a trend, so the next view uses a common yardstick. It tracks the year-over-year change in hectares lost to wildfire, comparing Canada and Russia with the United States as a baseline. Positive values mean a country lost more forest than the year before; negative values mean less.

The U.S. line barely leaves the center of the chart. Its largest annual swing in either direction is under 700,000 hectares, and its total loss in any single year stays below about one million. That makes it a useful control: a large country with substantial forests and well-publicized fire seasons, yet a relatively stable record.

Canada and Russia break from that pattern in different ways. Russia's story is one of volatility at a high level. Since 2012, its yearly swings regularly top 1 million hectares, including a rise of roughly 2.1 million that year and a drop of roughly 2.2 million in 2022, as it settled into a range that has mostly run between 2 and 5 million hectares lost annually. Canada's record, in contrast, was relatively flat for two decades, with annual losses mostly between 1 and 2 million hectares, until 2023, when its total jumped by about 6.3 million hectares in one year. Its loss fell by about 3.3 million the following year, yet 2024's 4.4 million hectares was still more than double the typical Canadian year before 2023.

What this suggests about a warming climate

Scientists widely link a warming climate to longer, hotter and drier fire seasons, conditions that leave northern forests primed to burn. The pivot table fits that expectation in an important way: the change is not a gradual drift but a pattern of larger and more extreme swings, which is how a system under growing stress would be expected to behave. The largest single-year jumps in the record, Canada's in 2023 and Russia's in 2016 and 2012, sit in a period of rising global temperatures, while the comparison country shows no comparable surge.

The data alone cannot prove cause. Tree cover loss reflects weather in a given year, how much fuel a forest holds, and how fires are managed, as well as climate. But taken together, the record of the past two decades gives the heavy media attention a firm footing: the largest wildfire losses on record are recent, and they are concentrated where the climate is changing fastest.

About the data

The map and pivot table measure tree cover loss as a raw total in hectares, not as a share of each country's land. Larger countries naturally lose more, so Canada and Russia may look worse partly because of their size. Measuring loss as a percentage of land area could reveal smaller countries that are disproportionately affected by wildfire even when their totals are small. That is a natural next step for this research.

Methods: Annual tree cover loss (hectares) attributed to wildfires was downloaded from Our World in Data for 2001–2024. The pivot table was built in Excel from the same data for the USA, Russia and Canada, subtracting each year's wildfire loss from the next to show the year-over-year change. Values are totals in hectares, not percentages of land area.
